# 2026 School Mathematics Competition prize-giving talk

## Event and audience

- Speaker: Jiadong Mao. Occasion: University of Melbourne School Mathematics Competition prize-giving, Saturday 17 October 2026, 3–4 pm, JH Michell Theatre, Peter Hall Building.
- Three speakers each give a 5–10 minute talk and then present prizes for one division. Jiadong's assigned division has not yet been recorded here.
- Audience: prize-winning and aspiring secondary-school students (Years 7–12), teachers and parents. The talk should reward mathematical curiosity while remaining understandable without biology or statistics background.
- Target **8–9 minutes of speech** within the 10-minute slot (Jiadong, 17 October 2026: running slightly longer is acceptable), leaving room for transitions to the prize handover. Keep the tone celebratory, curious and concrete rather than clinical or alarming.

## Agreed direction

Working title: **The hidden family trees in your blood**.

Central question: How can we reconstruct the ancestry of blood cells that we cannot watch dividing? Use a small audience puzzle to introduce inherited DNA changes, then show how mathematical reasoning and statistics help us study how one cell family can come to make a bigger share of someone's blood. Mention Jiadong's own work on how blood rebuilds after a transplant in one spoken sentence only, and explain why the research could matter in practice.

The takeaway is that mathematics helps us draw and test explanations from incomplete clues. Keep one main mystery rather than offering a survey of haematology.

## Story (human opener, six beats and a bridge; opener added 17 October 2026)

Times follow the current speaker notes, estimated at 135 words per minute.

| Time | Slide / purpose | Content and audience action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–1:00 | Who is your closest relative? (human family tree) | Grandparents, parents, aunt and uncle, you, a sibling and two cousins. Ask: sibling or cousin? Reveal that closer relatives share a *more recent* ancestor, then: people keep names, photos and memories, but cells keep no records, only clues. Partners who married in are drawn in grey. The notes add, if asked, that a cell has only one parent. |
| 1:00–1:40 | The hidden family tree | Open with apparently similar white blood cells and ask which have a recent common ancestor. |
| 1:40–2:40 | A blood-making family | Show a simple branching diagram: stem cells make descendants; a *clone* is a group descended from one cell. Some DNA changes are inherited and can serve as lineage clues. |
| 2:40–4:55 | Solve the tree (two slides: puzzle, then tree counting) | Four toy cells share coloured marks: all share one; A and B share another; C and D share a third; B and D each have a private mark. Ask the audience to group the closest relatives, then reveal the tree. Deeper point at the reveal: only *shared* marks show who is related; a private mark only shows that a cell kept dividing on its own. Then: with 4 cells there are only 15 possible family trees, but with 20 cells there are about 8 × 10²¹, so nobody can check them all. The marks are a deliberately simplified example. |
| 4:55–5:45 | Bridge: what real trees show | Two simplified trees: a younger adult's (many small families) and an over-75's (a few big families). Gives the reason to draw trees and sets up the question "why did a few families get so big?". Uses the Mitchell 2022 abstract figures, moved here from the research-loop slide. Notes include one sentence that a few typos *might* change cell behaviour, framed as an open question. |
| 5:45–6:45 | A percentage puzzle | A toy clone rises from 25% to 50%. Ask whether it necessarily made more cells. Reveal that its count could stay at 25 while the other cells fall from 75 to 25. Distinguish a fraction from an absolute count. Close by linking back: a bigger share in older people does not yet tell us whether those families grew or the others faded. |
| 6:45–7:50 | What researchers do | Grow this out of the two puzzles: shares alone cannot tell us what happened, and there are too many trees to check by hand. So we look at the clues → guess the rules → run the rules forward → check against what we see, and repeat. Use plain verbs, not "model", "simulate" or "infer". The right-hand box, "Our question", lists three candidate stories: the big families grew faster, the others faded, or a DNA typo helped them. |
| 7:50–9:00 | Why it matters / close | Understanding how cell families grow can inform research into blood ageing, recovery after a transplant and patterns linked to blood-cancer risk. One spoken sentence on Jiadong's own transplant question, without collaborator results. Return to the competition's theme of finding explanations from sparse clues. |

The tree puzzle is the main interactive moment; allow roughly 20 seconds for students to think before revealing the answer. The percentage puzzle provides a second, quick mathematical surprise. Use sparse slide text, large diagrams and speaker notes for the fuller explanation. Avoid equations and unexplained acronyms on screen.

## Plain language (agreed 16 October 2026)

Minimise jargon. The whole talk has a vocabulary budget of three words to define: **DNA**, **stem cell** and **clone** (on screen, prefer "cell family"). Introduce each with a picture before using it, and put no more than one new word on any slide. Technical terms may appear in speaker notes or small citations for teachers, never in headlines.

| Avoid on screen | Say instead |
| --- | --- |
| haematopoiesis / clonal haematopoiesis | how your body makes blood; one cell family making a bigger share of the blood |
| lineage, phylogeny | family tree |
| somatic mutation | a DNA "typo" that is copied into every descendant |
| haematopoietic stem cell | blood-making cell (in your bone marrow) |
| proportion / fraction vs absolute count | share vs number |
| model, simulate, infer | guess the rules, run them forward, check against reality |
| DNMT3A, barcodes, donor/recipient | leave out |

## Scientific and presentation guardrails

- Use **white blood cells** or stem-cell-derived samples when discussing DNA sequencing. Mature human red blood cells lack nuclei and should not be depicted as the cells whose nuclear DNA is sequenced.
- Distinguish naturally occurring DNA changes used to reconstruct human cell ancestry from deliberately introduced **barcodes** used to track lineages in experiments. Explain this in one plain sentence if both appear.
- A clone is a lineage, not automatically a cancer. A larger observed fraction does not by itself establish faster intrinsic growth, a larger absolute cell count or a particular biological mechanism.
- Risk associations are population findings, not a diagnosis or individual prediction. Do not suggest this research already guides a particular clinical decision without a verified source.
- The transplantation collaboration concerns mouse donor age and DNMT3A, with preliminary observations. Decision (16 October 2026): it does not get a slide; mention it in at most one plain spoken sentence as an open question; do not show or assert unpublished outcomes without confirmation. Keep donor-versus-recipient contribution separate from proportions among donor clones.
- Define biology before using mathematical language. Make the puzzle solvable for younger students, then add one deeper inference or limitation for older students and teachers. Give parents a concrete account of why the work matters.
- Use real data or figures only when their meaning, permission and attribution are checked. Mark invented counts and diagrams as illustrative. Cite the source of substantive factual claims unobtrusively on slides or in speaker notes.
